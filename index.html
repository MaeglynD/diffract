The fields

This experiment solves a monochromatic, lossless boundary problem. The field is invariant along the vertical axis. A finite-height window exposes its three-dimensional electric and magnetic vectors; fading at the viewing edges is graphical only. The surfaces show dielectric boundaries or a cavity cutaway, rather than an aperture that emits a prescribed field.

Energy and observation

Energy shows the dielectric-weighted mean energy density in the volume. On the observation plane it shows forward axial power flux for the guides and tunnelling experiment, and mean energy density for the circulating cavity. The latter has no single forward direction. The displayed equation identifies the sampled quantity. Signed field shows the real electric component; phase shows its complex angle, weighted by amplitude. The optical cycle advances the monochromatic field, while the detector scan moves the observation plane independently.

The two guide supermodes carry equal integrated power. About 97% of the combined axial power occupies one half-space at each transfer maximum; the residual field in the other guide is part of the exact solution. In the tunnelling model, interference between both evanescent terms transports energy across the gap. A single decaying term alone would carry no normal mean power.

Numerical representation

Analytical fields are evaluated locally in a worker at full resolution, then stored as complex binary16 textures. There is no coarse-to-fine field swap. Spacing is at most transversely and axially; identical vertical rows represent exact vertical invariance. The carrier is restored analytically after interpolation. Material boundaries and the conducting wall have finite display resolution. The tests independently check Maxwell’s curl equations, interface continuity, reflected/transmitted power and the guide transfer length.

The fields

The description above specifies this instrument's boundary model. The microscope and Fabry–Pérot use a two-dimensional angular spectrum, with longitudinal electric field and all three magnetic components. Forward and backward waves have their own vector relations. The Michelson, multilayer, graded-index glass and absorbing film use vertically invariant TE fields, drawn inside a finite-height viewing window.

These are monochromatic steady-state fields. Bright volumes reveal the computed field; they are not scattered light visible in otherwise empty vacuum. Hardware outside the stated optical boundaries is illustrative. Thin-lens transmissions, ideal mirrors and the beam splitter do not simulate their microscopic coatings or edge scattering.

Energy and phase

The volume shows mean energy density in Energy mode. The microscope observation plane shows forward power flux; the other instruments show energy density, including the local permittivity in glass. Electric field is signed and evolves through the optical cycle. Phase is the complex angle of the vertical electric component, with darkness indicating weak amplitude.

Calculation

The field is generated locally at its final resolution. The microscope propagates through each lens and the Fourier-plane phase dot in sequence; no specimen image is pasted onto the detector. The Fabry–Pérot sums the infinite reflection series analytically for every retained angular component. The multilayer matches tangential fields at each dielectric interface. The Michelson conserves power at its ideal scattering junction; its moving mirror changes every angular component’s round-trip path. The graded-index glass sums exact Helmholtz modes; the absorbing film matches the electric field and the magnetic-field jump from its surface current.

Numerical model

Each curated experiment is propagated ahead of time on a angular-spectrum grid spanning . The full complex fields are stored throughout a three-dimensional volume. The detector samples their real and imaginary parts directly on the GPU at its current position; there is no queued recalculation while dragging.

The reconstructed transverse grid aligns with the propagation grid at , with axial spacing . Float32 inverse FFT arithmetic, binary16 storage and trilinear interpolation introduce finite numerical error. The common carrier is removed before interpolation and restored analytically. An aligned half-resolution preview appears first and crossfades to the full field; cached full fields open directly. Compact angular spectra are reconstructed in a worker, retaining every propagated mode. The original stored fields provide a compatibility fallback. Full textures upload in small groups of depth layers. Energy ray marching uses a cached scalar density with a local interpolation correction; sampled RMS differences from direct propagation are below across these experiments. Detector flux and probe values are formed from the complex fields.

This is a band-limited aperture/launch-field model. Material scattering, reflections, thickness, edge charges, and evanescent waves are excluded. The optical elements are schematic representations of the prescribed transmission. Helicon, petal, caustic and vortex phase elements show an equivalent wrapped relief with wavelengths at index contrast , with separate attenuation and a pupil stop. The bunny, five-focus and flat-image holograms use thin patterned displays representing ideal complex-amplitude modulation. They are not glass sculptures. Binary apertures are openings in an absorbing screen; the screen’s graphical transparency does not change its transmission or the computed field. The coloured mask preview in this information view encodes phase delay by hue and transmission amplitude by brightness. These are schematic encodings, not a material simulation. The axicon cone illustrates the equivalent thin-phase profile for an index contrast of 0.5. Colour palettes affect the signed-field and energy volumes and both detectors. In optical-phase view, changing palette rotates the cyclic hue reference.
